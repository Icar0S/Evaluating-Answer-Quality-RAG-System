\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Context.} Intelligent software assistants built on retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG) reached production before a practice of testing them matured.
The suites written for them pair questions with reference answers and judge the
generated text, whereas the documented failure points of RAG concentrate in the
data and retrieval stages that precede generation.
\textbf{Objectives.} To determine which layers of a RAG pipeline produce defects
that a representative test suite fails to detect, how much the reported
adequacy depends on the oracle that produced it, and what each level of rigour
costs.
\textbf{Methods.} We derive a catalogue of 18 mutation operators spanning
corpus, chunking, indexing, retrieval and prompt from documented RAG failure
points, each with an executable fidelity check, and apply it to an open-source
software-engineering RAG assistant with a 30-case suite built by
equivalence-class partitioning. Five oracles of different natures --- cosine
similarity, deterministic assertions, a RAGAS metric bundle, an LLM judge and
their conjunction --- read the same execution log of about 3,000 invocations.
Non-determinism is discounted by restricting every denominator to the cases that
are stable in the unmutated baseline, and cost is measured per invocation in
wall-clock time, tokens, GPU-seconds and energy.
\textbf{Results.} \todo{3--4 frases COM OS NUMEROS: escore de mutacao por
camada, swing entre oraculos, fracao de mortes coerentes e custo por mutante
morto em cada nivel de rigor.} Two oracle biases were established before the
campaign: on this corpus the cosine-similarity oracle ranks injected numeric
errors and inverted conditions \emph{above} correct paraphrases (AUC 0.25 and
0.09), and the faithfulness metric of the RAGAS bundle scores a fabricated
figure about an absent topic as fully faithful.
\textbf{Conclusion.} \todo{2 frases. O que o leitor leva para casa.} An
adequacy score for a RAG assistant is a property of the pair (suite, oracle),
and must be reported as such.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Intelligent software assistants have moved out of the prototype stage and into
the daily practice of software engineering. Engineers now consult them to locate
a rule in a specification, explain the behaviour of a framework, draft a test
case or justify a design decision. Most of these assistants are built on
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)~\cite{lewis2020rag}: instead of answering
from what the model memorised during training, the system retrieves passages
from a controlled corpus and conditions the answer on them. The architecture is
attractive precisely because it promises grounding --- answers traceable to
documents the organisation owns and can update --- and it has become the
default way of putting a language model to work over proprietary or fast-moving
knowledge.

That promise carries an obligation. An assistant that answers questions about
an interface contract, a regulatory constraint or a test procedure is part of
the engineering process, and a wrong answer does not stop at the screen: it
propagates into the artefacts engineers produce from it. These systems
nevertheless reached production faster than the practice of testing them
matured.

Where testing does exist, it has a recognisable shape. A suite pairs questions
with reference answers, runs them against the deployed system, and derives a
verdict by comparing each generated answer to its reference, sometimes
reinforced by textual assertions or by a second model acting as a judge. This is
the shape reported in industrial experiences with deployed assistants, including
our own~\cite{oliveira2026sast}, and it is the shape a practitioner naturally
reaches for, because the answer is what the user sees.

The difficulty is that the answer is not where most failures originate. Barnett
et al.~\cite{barnett2024seven} catalogue seven failure points observed when
engineering RAG systems, and four of the seven occur before a single token is
generated: content missing from the corpus, the relevant passage never
retrieved, the passage retrieved but crowded out of the context window, the
passage present but not extracted. A suite that inspects only the final answer
observes a five-stage pipeline through its narrowest aperture. Whether it
detects what happens upstream is an open question --- and one that, to our
knowledge, no study has posed.

Posing it means asking whether a given suite is \emph{adequate}. For
deterministic software the standard instrument is mutation
testing~\cite{demillo1978hints,jia2011analysis}: inject small faults into the
system, run the suite against each faulty version, and measure how many are
detected. Transposing that instrument to a RAG assistant meets two obstacles.

The first is that existing adaptations do not reach the layer where the failures
are. Mutation has been applied to the system prompt of an LLM-based
chatbot~\cite{braga2026mutation} and used to assess the adequacy of
LLM-generated unit tests~\cite{cabral2026prompt}; metamorphic testing has been
applied to the prompt that triggers tool-calling agents~\cite{amorim2026trigger}.
Each perturbs the instruction given to the model, or the code the model writes.
None perturbs the corpus, the chunking strategy, the index or the retrieval
parameters --- that is, none perturbs the retrieval pipeline that distinguishes
a RAG system from a chatbot.

The second obstacle is that mutation score rests on an assumption that RAG
violates. A mutant is said to be killed when the suite fails on it, and the score
is only meaningful if the failure is attributable to the injected fault. A RAG
assistant returns different answers to the same question across runs, so a test
may fail for reasons that have nothing to do with the mutation. The classical
remedy --- identifying equivalent mutants by checking whether the mutant behaves
exactly like the original --- is unavailable here, because the original is
itself unstable. Any adequacy measure for these systems must therefore separate
the effect of the injected fault from the noise of the system under test, and
must do so before, not after, reporting a score.

A third obstacle emerged while we were building the instruments for this study,
and it changes what an adequacy score can mean. The verdict that decides whether
a mutant was killed is produced by an oracle, and oracles for natural-language
answers disagree with one another. Calibrating the cheapest and most widely
deployed of them --- cosine similarity between the generated answer and its
reference --- we found that on our corpus no threshold separates a correct
paraphrase from an injected factual error (AUC $=0.485$, below chance). The
effect is not noise but inversion: a sentence whose meaning had been reversed
scored 0.9912 against the reference, while a faithful paraphrase of that same
reference scored 0.9907, and a version differing only in one numeric value
scored 0.9184. Broken down by fault type, the oracle ranks injected errors
\emph{above} correct paraphrases for inverted conditions (AUC $=0.091$) and
swapped numbers (AUC $=0.248$), and the pattern replicates across two embedding
models from different families. The reason is structural: an injected error is
the reference with one token changed, and so remains textually almost
identical, whereas a legitimate paraphrase is a rewrite. Cosine similarity
measures surface proximity, not semantic equivalence, and therefore prefers the
minimal corruption to the honest rewording. A second oracle showed a
complementary blind spot: the faithfulness metric of the standard RAGAS
bundle~\cite{es2024ragas} scored 1.0 for an answer that fabricated a figure
about a topic entirely absent from the retrieved context, because a claim about
an absent topic contradicts nothing.

These are not incidental measurement problems. They mean that an adequacy score
reported without naming the oracle that produced it is not interpretable, in the
same way that a repair-effectiveness score depends on the validator that judged
the patches~\cite{bonfim2026evaluator}. This study therefore reports adequacy
per oracle rather than as a single number.

This paper addresses the three obstacles in turn. Against the first, we derive
a catalogue of mutation operators that spans the whole RAG pipeline --- corpus,
chunking, indexing, retrieval and prompt --- from documented failure points
rather than from intuition, and we verify each operator against a probe that
fails on the unmutated system and passes on the mutant, so that an operator
cannot appear to work for the wrong reason. Against the second, we restrict
every measurement to the subset of test cases whose verdict is stable across
repeated runs of the unmutated system, and report the residual instability
alongside the score. Against the third, we evaluate the same execution log with
five oracles of different natures and report the spread between them.

The system under test is an open-source RAG assistant whose own domain is the
testing of LLM- and RAG-based systems. The choice is deliberate on two counts:
it is an assistant of the software engineering lifecycle, which is the class of
system this special issue concerns, and it is public in its entirety, corpus
included, so that the study can be replicated rather than merely described. We
ask three questions: which layers of the pipeline produce defects the suite
fails to detect (RQ1), how much the reported adequacy depends on the oracle
applied to identical artefacts (RQ2), and what each level of rigour costs, in
wall-clock time, tokens, GPU-seconds and energy (RQ3).

In earlier work we reported an experience with testing a deployed
retrieval-augmented assistant in the public sector~\cite{oliveira2026sast}. That
short report described a test architecture for one industrial system and did
not assess the adequacy of the resulting suite. The present study asks a
different question over a different system: what such suites fail to detect,
and how to find out.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(1)] A catalogue of \textbf{18 mutation operators for RAG pipelines}
  spanning corpus, chunking, indexing, retrieval and prompt, each derived from a
  documented RAG failure point and each accompanied by an executable fidelity
  check that fails on the baseline and passes on the mutant
  (Section~\ref{sec:operators}).
\item[(2)] An \textbf{adequacy protocol under non-determinism} that separates
  the effect of an injected defect from the intrinsic instability of the system
  under test, by restricting every denominator to the cases that are stable in
  the unmutated baseline (Sections~\ref{sec:equivalence}
  and~\ref{sec:design}).
\item[(3)] A \textbf{quantification of oracle bias} in RAG evaluation: five
  oracles applied to identical artefacts, with the adequacy score reported per
  oracle and two of the biases established before the campaign rather than
  inferred from it (Sections~\ref{sec:oracles} and~\ref{sec:rq2}).
\item[(4)] A \textbf{cost characterisation per level of rigour} --- wall-clock
  time, tokens, GPU-seconds and estimated energy --- normalised by mutants
  killed (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
\item[(5)] A \textbf{complete replication package} that runs on consumer
  hardware with open-weight local models, released under an open-source
  license, comprising the system under test, the corpus, the suite, the
  operators, the raw execution log and the analysis scripts (see the Data
  availability statement).
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews RAG failure points, the oracle problem in
LLM-based software, mutation testing for such systems and the evaluation of
intelligent assistants, and states the gap. Section~\ref{sec:context} describes
the system under test, its corpus and the test suite.
Section~\ref{sec:operators} presents the operator catalogue and the treatment of
equivalence. Section~\ref{sec:oracles} describes the five oracles and the
calibration of the similarity threshold. Section~\ref{sec:design} sets out the
study design and the analysis procedure. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the
results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses their implications and
Section~\ref{sec:threats} the threats to validity.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Retrieval-augmented generation and its failure points}
\label{sec:rag}

Retrieval-augmented generation~\cite{lewis2020rag} separates what a system
knows from what its model memorised. A corpus of documents is segmented into
chunks, each chunk is embedded into a vector space, and at query time the
chunks closest to the embedded question are placed in the prompt that
conditions generation. The pipeline therefore has five distinguishable stages
--- corpus ingestion, chunking, indexing, retrieval and generation --- and a
defect can be introduced at any of them without producing an error at any
other.

Barnett et al.~\cite{barnett2024seven} report seven failure points observed
while engineering RAG systems in practice. Four of them arise before the model
writes anything: the answer is simply absent from the corpus (FP1); the passage
that contains it exists but is not ranked highly enough to be retrieved (FP2);
it is retrieved but does not survive consolidation into the context window
(FP3); or it reaches the context and is not extracted from it (FP4). The
remaining three concern the generated text itself: wrong output format (FP5),
an answer at the wrong level of specificity (FP6), and an incomplete answer
(FP7).

This distribution has a practical consequence that motivates the present study.
A test suite for a RAG assistant is typically written as a set of questions
paired with reference answers, and its verdict is derived from the generated
text. Such a suite observes FP5 to FP7 directly, because they are properties of
that text. It observes FP1 to FP4 only when they happen to change the answer in
a way the oracle notices --- and a retrieval fault often changes the answer
subtly, by removing a qualifier or a figure, rather than visibly. Industrial
reports of testing data-intensive and assistant systems describe suites of
exactly this shape~\cite{cavalcante2025bigdata,oliveira2026sast}, which suggests
the asymmetry is not an artefact of one project.

\subsection{The oracle problem in LLM-based software}
\label{sec:oracleproblem}

Deciding whether an output is correct is the oracle problem, and
natural-language answers make it acute: there is no single correct string, many
formulations are equally valid, and the same input does not yield the same
output twice. The last point holds even under configurations that are
nominally deterministic. Atil et al.~\cite{atil2025nondeterminism} show that
temperature zero and a fixed seed do not eliminate variation across runs, which
means that a failing test is not, by itself, evidence of a defect.

Dobslaw et al.~\cite{dobslaw2026challenges} organise the challenges of testing
LLM-based software into a faceted taxonomy, and surveys of the
field~\cite{wang2024testing} map the techniques that have been brought to bear.
Two gaps in that literature bear directly on this study. The first is
granularity: the taxonomy treats the LLM-based system as the unit under test,
without sub-faceting the retrieval layer, so the dimensions that a RAG
practitioner actually configures --- corpus version, chunk size, embedding
model, top-$k$, relevance floor --- do not appear as facets to control. The
second is cost: the taxonomy enumerates what can be tested and how, but not
what the testing costs in time, money or energy, even though repeated execution
is the only available answer to non-determinism and repetition is precisely
what costs.
\nota{Confira a caracterizacao da taxonomia. Se ela tratar custo em algum
lugar, a frase acima precisa ser suavizada -- lacuna mal caracterizada e o tipo
de afirmacao que o revisor checa primeiro.}

In practice, four families of oracle are used. Cosine similarity between the
embedding of the answer and that of a reference answer is the cheapest and the
most common; it requires one embedding call and no model inference.
Deterministic assertions over the answer text --- presence or absence of a
term, a regular expression, a citation marker --- are cheaper still, but cover
only what can be stated as a surface property. Reference-free metric bundles
such as RAGAS~\cite{es2024ragas} decompose the answer into claims and check each
against the retrieved context, and have become the de facto standard for RAG
evaluation. Finally, a second model can be used as a judge~\cite{zheng2023judging},
at the cost of importing that model's own inconsistency and its documented
preference for text produced by models of its own
family~\cite{panickssery2024llm}.

Each of these carries assumptions that are rarely stated. The similarity oracle
assumes that semantic equivalence is well approximated by proximity in
embedding space, an assumption that adjacent evidence already puts in doubt:
LLM-based representations capture surface regularities of code far better than
they capture its semantics~\cite{infsof2025codesemantics}. The metric bundles
assume that their notion of faithfulness --- whether claims contradict the
context, or whether they are inferable from it --- matches the notion of
correctness the tester has in mind. Section~\ref{sec:calibration} reports what
happens when these assumptions are examined instead of inherited.

\subsection{Mutation testing for LLM-based systems}
\label{sec:mutation}

Mutation testing answers a question that coverage does not: given a suite that
passes, would it notice if the system were wrong? Small faults are injected
into the system, the suite is run against each faulty version, and the
proportion detected --- the mutation score --- estimates the suite's
fault-detection ability~\cite{demillo1978hints}. The technique rests on two
assumptions~\cite{jia2011analysis}: that a killed mutant was killed by the
injected fault, and that mutants which no suite can kill are equivalent to the
original and can be identified by comparing behaviours. Both assumptions are
stable in deterministic software and neither survives contact with a RAG
assistant.

Three recent studies transpose fault injection to LLM-based systems, and
together they delimit what is missing. Braga and Paiva~\cite{braga2026mutation}
apply mutation operators to the system prompt of an LLM-based chatbot and
report the consequence of the first assumption failing: a raw mutation score of
0.86 falls to 0.38 once each kill is inspected and only those attributable to
the injected defect are counted. Their system under test has no retrieval layer,
and their campaign stops at the first killing test case, which hides the
remaining causes of death. Cabral et al.~\cite{cabral2026prompt} use mutation to
assess unit tests generated by LLMs under five prompting strategies, repeating
each generation five times to absorb non-determinism and diagnosing surviving
mutants to derive recommendations; there, however, the system under test is
deterministic Java code and only the test generator is stochastic. Amorim et
al.~\cite{amorim2026trigger} take a different route to the same obstacle:
rather than injecting faults, they apply metamorphic relations to the prompt of
tool-calling agents, and introduce an identity relation whose sole purpose is
to measure how much the agent varies without any transformation, so that this
intrinsic instability can be discounted before any violation is attributed to
a transformation.

The three converge on the prompt and on the code. None perturbs the corpus, the
chunking strategy, the index or the retrieval parameters --- that is, none
perturbs the stages where Section~\ref{sec:rag} locates four of the seven
documented failure points.

\subsection{Evaluating intelligent software assistants}
\label{sec:assistants}

Assistants built on language models are now studied as software artefacts in
their own right. Within this journal, Muttillo et al.~\cite{muttillo2025modeling}
address intelligent modeling assistants and the difficulty of obtaining the
data needed to evaluate them, and work on requirements engineering with LLMs
frames correctness and trustworthiness of generated artefacts as the central
concern~\cite{infsof2025reroadmap}; LLMs have likewise been applied to testing
artefacts themselves, such as the prioritisation of crowdsourced test
reports~\cite{ling2025crowdsourced}. What this line of work establishes is the
object; what it leaves open is how the verdict about that object is reached.

Three results sharpen that question. Bonfim and
Teixeira~\cite{bonfim2026evaluator} hold the artefacts fixed --- the same 300
program-repair patches --- and vary only the validator, obtaining a swing of
roughly twenty percentage points in reported effectiveness; the reported
quality of a system is therefore partly a property of the instrument that
measured it. Lima et al.~\cite{lima2026limits} report a delimiting result in
which LLM-based test prioritisation does not outperform lightweight lexical
baselines, and treat cost as a first-class outcome rather than a footnote, a
stance also taken by benchmark tooling for context-provisioning
strategies~\cite{vasconcelos2026llmcontextbench}. Verg\'ilio et
al.~\cite{vergilio2026rag} show that RAG gains measured on curated snippets do
not transfer to repository scale, where cost and maintainability become
binding.

Two observations from industrial practice complete the picture. Fran\c{c}a et
al.~\cite{franca2026genai} report that a RAG-based assistant identified seven
test scenarios for a flow in which human analysts identified forty-eight, and
attribute the gap to knowledge that exists in conversations and is never
written down --- a limit that no amount of retrieval tuning removes. And our own
experience with a deployed assistant in the public
sector~\cite{oliveira2026sast} produced a regression whose cause was an updated
regulatory document that remained indexed in its previous version: a corpus
fault, invisible to error monitoring, detected only because the answers
changed.

\subsection{Gap addressed by this study}
\label{sec:gap}

Taken together: the documented failure points of RAG concentrate in retrieval
and data; the suites written for these systems inspect the generated answer;
mutation testing, the standard instrument for asking whether a suite is
adequate, has been transposed to the prompt and to generated code but not to
the retrieval pipeline; the assumption that a kill is attributable to the
injected fault does not hold under non-determinism, and the classical route to
identifying equivalent mutants is unavailable because the unmutated system is
itself unstable; and the verdict that decides a kill comes from an oracle whose
assumptions are usually inherited rather than tested, even though changing the
oracle is known to change the reported score.

\emph{No study has yet asked which layers of a RAG pipeline produce defects
that its test suite fails to detect, nor how much of the answer depends on the
oracle used to obtain it.}

\section{Approach and Study Context}
\label{sec:context}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

The study proceeds in four stages. A suite of test cases is executed $N_b$
times against the unmutated assistant, which establishes which cases behave
stably enough to be measured at all. Each of 18 mutation operators is then
applied in turn, and the suite is executed $N_m$ times against each resulting
mutant. Every invocation is appended to a structured log. Only afterwards are
verdicts produced: five oracles read that log and decide, independently of one
another, whether each case passed. Adequacy, oracle disagreement and cost are
all computed from the resulting verdicts.

Three separations in the study infrastructure are worth stating, because each
rules out a class of error rather than merely organising code. \emph{Execution
is separated from judgement}: the campaign produces only the log of
invocations, and oracles are applied to that log afterwards. Recalibrating a
threshold, replacing an oracle implementation or re-running the judge therefore
costs no further GPU time, and the question ``what would a single-run protocol
have detected?'' can be answered without executing the assistant again.
\emph{The corpus is separated from the index}: the harness keeps what the
corpus contains and what was actually indexed in distinct locations, which
normally hold the same content --- operator E2 is the only thing that makes
them diverge, and that divergence is exactly the defect it injects.
\emph{Measurement is separated from decision}: the audit tools that check the
corpus, the operators and the suite report evidence and fail only on
unambiguous violations, leaving judgement calls to the researcher.

\subsection{The assistant under study}
\label{sec:sut}

The system under test is an open-source retrieval-augmented assistant whose
subject matter is the testing of LLM- and RAG-based systems. It is a public
reference implementation of the test architecture we reported for a deployed
public-sector assistant~\cite{oliveira2026sast}; the original system's corpus
and reference dataset contain operational information of the partner
organisation and cannot be released, which is what motivated building an
equivalent system that can. The choice therefore serves two purposes at once.
The assistant belongs to the software engineering lifecycle, which places it
within the class of system this study concerns; and it is public in its
entirety, which makes the replication package executable rather than
descriptive.

The pipeline runs entirely on local, open-weight models. Generation uses
\code{qwen3:8b} and embedding uses \code{nomic-embed-text}, both served through
Ollama; the vector store is ChromaDB; the backend is a FastAPI application
exposing endpoints for querying (\code{/chat}), re-ingesting the corpus
(\code{/ingest}) and reporting runtime metrics (\code{/metrics}). Every
interaction is appended to a structured log with the question, the retrieved
chunks, the answer and per-invocation metrics. The hardware is a consumer
laptop GPU (NVIDIA GeForce RTX~4060 Laptop, 8\,GB of VRAM).

Running locally is a methodological choice rather than a constraint of budget.
Commercial endpoints are revised without notice, which makes a campaign of
thousands of invocations unreproducible after the fact; open weights pinned by

it when discussing external validity.

The assistant already carried three test suites before this study: an API
suite, an end-to-end browser suite, and an answer-quality suite built on a
metric bundle of the kind described in Section~\ref{sec:o3}. None of them is
the object of this study. The suite under assessment here
(Section~\ref{sec:suite}) was built specifically for it, so that the classes of
question it covers could be controlled.

\subsection{Knowledge corpus}
\label{sec:corpus}

The corpus comprises 8 documents, 96 pages and approximately 164,000 tokens of
research literature on software testing. Two preparation decisions shape what
the study can observe, and both are consequences of how the assistant ingests
documents.

First, \emph{every} document is re-rendered from its extracted text, not only
the ones that operators modify. Were only mutated documents re-rendered, the
difference in extraction between an original and its mutated counterpart would
enter the results alongside the injected defect, and the two would be
indistinguishable.

Second, the re-rendering repaginates at a budget of roughly 1,800 tokens per
page. The assistant segments chunks at page boundaries, and a two-column
research paper yields pages of about 500 tokens; at the configured chunk size a
single chunk would then swallow an entire page, and the four chunking operators
would be inert by construction --- they would produce no observable change, and
the suite would appear to miss defects that the design never allowed it to see.
After repagination the index holds 184 chunks for 96 pages, or 1.9 chunks per
page, and the chunking operators have an effect that can be measured.

Text normalisation proved to matter more than expected. The Base-14 fonts used
by the source PDFs encode only Latin-1, so 1,372 symbols were extracted as
question marks, 677 of them in a single document --- and statistical notation
is precisely what the factual test cases quote. Unicode normalisation combined
with a substitution table for Greek letters and mathematical operators, applied
per character, recovered 93\% of them.

Three roles were assigned to documents so that the corpus-level operators have
targets: a \emph{primary} document dense in factual values (275 numeric or
named facts), from which the perturbed variants are derived; a \emph{secondary}
document whose final third is prose rather than bibliography, so that
truncation removes content the suite can ask about; and two \emph{recent}
documents, used by the operator that leaves the corpus and the index out of
step. Two variants of the primary document were produced, one representing a
superseded version and one introducing a conflicting value, each with 40
substitutions distributed across its pages; citation markers and bibliographic
entries were excluded from perturbation, because no test case asks about them.

The corpus is composed of research papers on software testing, and some of them
are related work of this very article. The overlap is a consequence of the
assistant's domain rather than a choice, and it does not enter the results:
every corpus-dependent test case asks about a factual value stated in a
document --- a count, a percentage, a threshold --- and none asks about its
thesis.

it derives. The 19 corpus-dependent cases were first drafted by the local model
from the indexed chunks and then verified one by one against the source; in
four rounds no number was invented, but the model regularly appended a
justification of its own to a correct fact (``93.7, \emph{since its
configuration optimises fidelity}'', where only the 93.7 is in the document).
Because the reference answer is itself the oracle, such a claim would penalise
a correct answer from the system; six references were rewritten to contain
only the literal.

Two further checks were added after early cases proved to fail for reasons
unrelated to what they were meant to test. The first verifies, for every case
that depends on the corpus, that the question actually retrieves the document
holding its evidence: in a corpus where every document discusses testing, a
generically worded question retrieves a topical neighbour instead, and the case
would then fail against the unmutated system and drop out of the analysis
silently. Three cases were rewritten with terms unique to their source
document; all 19 corpus-dependent cases now retrieve it. The second runs the
deterministic oracle against the reference answers themselves and discards any
assertion that its own reference does not satisfy --- an assertion that fails
on the baseline removes the case from the analysis just as quietly.

\subsection{Instrumentation}
\label{sec:instrumentation}

Mutation operators for a RAG pipeline change configuration rather than source
code, and several of the parameters they change did not exist as configurable
values in the assistant. Adding them required 264 lines of change, each
introduced with a default that preserves the previous behaviour: the system
prompt is emitted byte for byte as before, and the pre-existing API suite of 18
tests passes unchanged.

The harness never rewrites a file under the application. It applies a
configuration delta in memory and discards it when the mutant is reverted,
which eliminates the most expensive failure mode of a mutation campaign --- a
mutant that remained partially applied after its revert, silently contaminating
every subsequent measurement. The campaign also uses its own virtual
environment and its own vector index, so running it does not disturb the
assistant's operational state.

The study baseline is not the repository default, and each difference exists
so that an operator has something to remove: chunks of 1,200 tokens with an
overlap of 200 (repository: 800/120), because the chunking operators are
defined as deltas from those values; maximal-marginal-relevance re-ranking on
(repository: off), because R3 switches it off; a relevance floor above zero
(repository: 0.0), because R4 removes it; the citation instruction on
(repository: off), because P3 removes it; and temperature fixed at 0.0
explicitly, because the protocol forbids amplifying non-determinism anywhere
except in P4. The harness refuses to apply an operator whose override equals
the baseline value, so a mutant equivalent by configuration cannot enter the
campaign unnoticed.

One parameter of the model server had to be declared before any measurement
could be trusted, and it was not in the protocol. Without an explicit context
window the server uses 4,096 tokens and silently truncates every prompt longer
than half of it, keeping the first four tokens and the last 2,046 --- which
discards the system prompt, and with it the grounding, abstention and citation
rules. The first full baseline of this study (300 invocations) ran that way;
the server reported it only in its own log, and every oracle judged fluent
answers produced from parametric knowledge as if the rules had been
disobeyed. The window is now sized from the retrieval configuration of each
mutant, $\mathrm{num\_ctx} = \lceil (k \cdot s \cdot 1.25 + 1{,}024 +
\mathrm{num\_predict}) / 1{,}024 \rceil \cdot 1{,}024$ for top-$k$ and chunk
size $s$, with the generation cap explicit because the server's prompt limit
is their difference; a fixed window large enough for the widest mutant (12
chunks) would have forced CPU offloading on every invocation instead of only
on K2 and R2. The harness records the prompt length of every invocation and
turns a prompt at the limit into an error record. Section~\ref{sec:pilot}
reports the consequence for the schedule; Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
returns to it, because it is the failure point FP3 of Barnett et
al.~\cite{barnett2024seven} occurring by construction in the unmutated
system, invisible to a suite that judges the answer.

One baseline parameter had to be calibrated before the campaign could begin.
The retrieval relevance floor was a configured but unenforced value; the
operator that removes it can only have an effect if the baseline enforces one.
Choosing that floor exposed an asymmetry worth stating, because the same
reasoning governs the threshold calibrated in Section~\ref{sec:calibration}. A
floor set too high rejects a question that should have been answered, the case
then fails against the unmutated system, and it disappears from the denominator
of every research question without any error being raised. A floor set too low
merely lets an unanswerable question through, which the abstention instruction
is supposed to catch and which is therefore visible in the results. The two
errors do not cost the same, so the floor was placed with a margin of 0.02
below the weakest legitimate case (0.428) even though a slightly higher value
scored better on the criterion being optimised.

The calibration also produced a measurement that the results must be read
against. In its first version of the suite, 5 of the 8 negative cases (out of
corpus or out of scope) scored \emph{above} any floor that preserved every
positive case. The clearest was a question about the equivalent-mutant rate
that PIT reports on Defects4J, which had the highest similarity of the whole
suite (0.656) because the primary document is about PIT, Defects4J and
surviving mutants: the value is absent, the topic is densely covered. That
question and two others were replaced by questions further from the corpus,
and \todo{N} of the 8 negatives still clear the floor. In a topically
homogeneous corpus a global similarity floor does not separate ``covered'' from
``not covered'', so the out-of-corpus class exercises the abstention
instruction (P2) far more than the relevance floor (R4). We return to this in
Sections~\ref{sec:rq2} and~\ref{sec:threats}.

\section{Mutation Operators for RAG Pipelines}
\label{sec:operators}

\subsection{Derivation}
\label{sec:derivation}

The catalogue was derived from the failure points of
Section~\ref{sec:rag} rather than from what was convenient to perturb, under
five rules.

\emph{One defect per operator.} Each operator injects a single plausible fault
at a single stage of the pipeline and is traced to one failure point. An
operator that changed two things at once would produce kills that cannot be
attributed.

\emph{Each defect has a repair.} Two operators that look alike are kept apart
when a practitioner would fix them differently. C1 removes a document from the
corpus (repair: obtain the document again); E2 leaves the document in the
corpus and out of the index (repair: re-run ingestion). The distinction is
exactly the one that matters to whoever maintains the assistant.

\emph{Configuration, not code.} RAG defects are, overwhelmingly, configuration
and data defects: a chunk size chosen for another corpus, a re-indexing that
did not run, a relevance floor that was never enforced. Every operator is
therefore a delta over the assistant's configuration or over the files it
ingests, never an edit to its source. This also keeps the operator set
portable to any assistant that exposes the same parameters.

\emph{Two operators replicate incidents.} C2 (a superseded version of a
document remains indexed) and E2 (the corpus changed and the index did not) are
the two failures we observed in operation with a deployed public-sector
assistant~\cite{oliveira2026sast}. Their presence anchors the catalogue in what
actually breaks, not only in what could.

\emph{One control operator.} P4 raises the generation temperature from 0 to
0.8. It is the only operator without a failure point: it injects no error of
content, it injects instability. It exists to check the protocol itself --- if
the evaluable-set rule of Section~\ref{sec:equivalence} does what it claims, P4
should surface as a loss of stable verdicts rather than as coherent kills.

\subsection{Application, reversion and fidelity}
\label{sec:fidelity}

Applying an operator always starts from a pristine copy of the corpus and of
the index sources, so that no operator can inherit the residue of another.
Configuration operators are applied as an in-memory overlay on the assistant's
settings; corpus operators are file operations on the working copy. A gate,
run before the campaign, applies each operator, reverts it and compares the
working copy against the pristine signature: all 18 revert without residue.

Reverting without residue does not show that the mutant \emph{is} what the
catalogue says. Mutation studies of deterministic code rarely need to ask, but
a configuration delta can be accepted by the system and change nothing ---
because the parameter is not enforced, because the corpus has no content at
the point the operator hits, or because the baseline already had the value the
operator sets. We therefore attached to every operator an executable fidelity
check that observes the effect at the stage the operator is supposed to alter
(Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}). The criterion is the one any fidelity check should
satisfy: the probe must \emph{fail on the baseline and pass on the mutant}.

\subsection{Equivalence under non-determinism: the evaluable set}
\label{sec:equivalence}

In deterministic software an equivalent mutant is one that no test can
distinguish from the original, and it is detected by comparing behaviours. A
RAG assistant offers no fixed behaviour to compare against: the original
answers differently across runs, so ``behaves like the original'' has no single
witness. We replace the notion with one that can be computed.

Let $\Cset$ be the suite and $v_o(m, c, r) \in \{\pass, \fail\}$ the verdict of
oracle $o$ on case $c$ in run $r$ against system $m$, where $m = b$ denotes the
unmutated baseline. The \emph{evaluable set} of oracle $o$ is the set of cases
on which the baseline is both stable and correct across its $N_b$ runs:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:evaluable}
\Sset_o = \bigl\{\, c \in \Cset \;:\; v_o(b, c, r) = \pass \ \text{for all}\ r = 1, \dots, N_b \,\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Every denominator in the study is restricted to $\Sset_o$. A case that is
unstable in the baseline would produce kills that cannot be attributed to any
mutation; a case that fails stably in the baseline would fail on every mutant
and inflate the kill rate without carrying information about the injected
defect. The two exclusions are reported separately (Section~\ref{sec:evaluable}),
because they mean different things: the first measures the intrinsic
instability of the system, the second measures where the suite and the
unmutated system already disagree. Requiring the stable verdict to be
\emph{pass} is a refinement over the pre-registered protocol, which required
stability only; the protocol's version can be recovered from the log.

The evaluable set is per oracle, not global, because the same run can be
stable under a deterministic assertion and unstable under a judge. This is what
allows RQ2 to compare oracles on identical artefacts: each oracle is scored on
its own stable ground.

The set is also what separates a surviving mutant from a mutant that no case
could have killed. Three sources of ``equivalence by construction'' were found
and removed before the campaign, and each generalises beyond this study.
\emph{By corpus structure}: C3 truncates the last 30\% of a document, and in a
research paper the last third is almost always the reference list --- which no
test case asks about. Of the eight documents, the first measurement found only
one with prose in the region C3 deletes; the secondary role was assigned by
measuring the density of facts per page rather than by choice. \emph{By
baseline configuration}: R3, R4 and P3 remove something the repository default
never had (Section~\ref{sec:instrumentation}). \emph{By ingestion}: the four
chunking operators were inert on the original pagination
(Section~\ref{sec:corpus}). The observability of a corpus-level operator
depends on the internal structure of the document it acts on, and that
dependency does not appear in a catalogue; a catalogue of RAG operators
therefore has to travel with a criterion of corpus adequacy, or the study will
report ``the suite does not detect'' where the truth is ``the design did not
allow detection''.

\section{Oracles under Study}
\label{sec:oracles}

Every oracle is a function of the same four artefacts recorded in the log ---
the question, its reference answer, the generated answer and the retrieved
context --- and returns \pass{} or \fail{}. The oracles were chosen to span the
four families of Section~\ref{sec:oracleproblem} and to be the ones a
practitioner actually deploys, in the form in which they are deployed. Their
cost is deliberately asymmetric: the two cheap oracles (O1, O2) and their
conjunction (O5) are applied to every run, and the two expensive ones (O3, O4)
are applied once per (mutant, case) pair to the \emph{modal} answer of its
runs. The asymmetry reduces the number of judge evaluations from 13,500 to
2,700; it is part of the cost result of RQ3, not a shortcut hidden from it.

\subsection{O1 --- cosine similarity to the reference}
\label{sec:o1}

O1 passes when the cosine similarity between the embedding of the generated
answer and the embedding of the reference answer is at least a threshold
$\tau^\ast$, calibrated in Section~\ref{sec:calibration}. The embedding model
is fixed to \code{nomic-embed-text} regardless of the configuration of the
system under test; if the oracle followed the system's embedding model,
operator E1 would change the measuring instrument together with the system it
measures. O1 is the oracle of the answer-quality suites we have seen in
practice, including our own~\cite{oliveira2026sast}, and it is included in
that form.

\subsection{O2 --- deterministic assertions}
\label{sec:o2}

O2 is a set of assertions over the answer text, and only over the answer text.
The 30 cases carry 71 assertions of ten kinds: required terms
(\code{contains\_all}, \code{contains\_any}), forbidden terms
(\code{not\_contains}), a regular expression for a stated condition
(\code{regex}), a length bound (\code{max\_words}), the presence or absence of
the assistant's abstention phrase (\code{must\_abstain},
\code{must\_not\_abstain}), the refusal and clarification behaviours
(\code{must\_refuse}, \code{must\_ask\_clarification}) and, for the
traceability class, a citation of the source document and page
(\code{must\_cite\_document}, \code{must\_cite\_page}). O2 never asserts over
the retrieved chunks. The restriction is deliberate: the hypothesis behind RQ1
is that a typical suite tests the output and not the retrieval, and a suite
already instrumented with retrieval assertions would answer RQ1 by
construction.

Every assertion was validated against its own reference answer before the
campaign, and any assertion the reference does not satisfy was discarded
(Section~\ref{sec:suite}). The check caught three cases in the ambiguous class
whose references described a request for clarification instead of making one,
and one false positive of the opposite kind, a condition pattern that matched
inside an unrelated word until word boundaries were enforced. A hand-written
oracle that approves by accident is as harmful as one that rejects by
accident, and neither is visible unless the oracle is run against the baseline
first.

\subsection{O3 --- reference-free metric bundle}
\label{sec:o3}

O3 applies the three RAGAS metrics that a RAG evaluation typically reports
--- faithfulness, answer relevancy and context precision~\cite{es2024ragas} ---
each computed by a local judge model over the answer and the retrieved
context, and passes when all three reach 0.7. The threshold and the
conjunction were fixed in the protocol before any data existed, so that O3 is
the metric bundle as a project would deploy it as a gate. Two implementations of
the metrics were available, the reference implementation named in the protocol
and a second library declared as the alternative; the choice between them was a
pre-registered decision point of the pilot, with no automatic fallback, because
an O3 that passes by failing to measure would contaminate RQ2 entirely. What
the pilot found is reported in Section~\ref{sec:pilot}.

\subsection{O4 --- LLM as a judge}
\label{sec:o4}

O4 asks a second model whether the generated answer is acceptable given the
question and the reference answer. Three mitigations bound what the judge can
be blamed for. The judge (\code{gemma3:4b}) is from a different model family
than the generator (\code{qwen3:8b}), against the documented preference of
evaluators for text of their own family~\cite{panickssery2024llm}. Each pair is
judged five times at temperature 0 with seeds 1 to 5 and decided by simple
majority, against the judge's own instability; at temperature 0 the five
judgements measure the residual non-determinism of the runtime rather than
sampling variance, and if the five always agree that is a datum about the
judge, not a wasted budget. The judge answers in a constrained JSON schema with
a verdict, a predominant cause from a fixed list and a two-sentence
justification, so that its output can be parsed without interpretation. The
bias that remains after the three mitigations is what RQ2 measures.

One instruction in the judge's prompt was added after the pilot and is itself
a finding. The judge does not see the source document; it sees the question,
the reference and the answer. It therefore cannot know whether a detail absent
from the reference is true, and can only judge whether the answer
\emph{contradicts} the reference. The first version of the prompt asked for
``no unsupported claim'', and the judge read \emph{unsupported} as \emph{absent
from the reference}: a correct answer more detailed than its reference --- the
same three percentages, each described by its cause rather than by the name of
the mutator --- failed for ``adding information''. The prompt now states that
the reference is the minimum an acceptable answer must contain, not the
maximum. After the change, controls behaved as required: a swapped number and
a swapped mutator still fail, a shorter correct answer passes. A reference-based
judge carries a structural bias against answers \emph{better} than their
reference, and that bias only becomes visible when the system under test is
good.

\subsection{O5 --- conjunction}
\label{sec:o5}

O5 is the conjunction of O1 and all assertions of O2:
$\mathrm{O5}(c) = \mathrm{O1}(c) \wedge \bigwedge_i \mathrm{O2}_i(c)$. It is
not ``oracles in parallel'': a deterministic violation fails the case whatever
the similarity score, and a semantic miss fails it whatever the assertions
say. O5 is the form a practitioner arrives at after the first false pass of a
similarity oracle, and it is the primary oracle of the pre-registered analysis
for RQ1; the other four are reported alongside it.

\subsection{Calibration of the similarity threshold}
\label{sec:calibration}

A similarity threshold chosen by intuition is an undeclared oracle. The
protocol therefore fixed a calibration procedure before any campaign data
existed: build a labelled set of answer pairs, sweep $\tau \in [0.60, 0.95]$
in steps of 0.01, and take the $\tau^\ast$ that maximises $F_1$ for the class
of correct answers, with a bootstrap confidence interval.

\paragraph{Pairs.} Each pair is a reference answer and a candidate. Candidates
of class \textsc{equiv} are paraphrases of the reference produced by a model of
a different family from the generator (\code{gemma3:4b}) and reviewed
individually: all 115 paraphrases were read, 111 approved and 4 rejected, for
degeneration (``Pitest (Pitest) was adopted'') or for an altered relation
(``stable across seeds'' becoming ``stable when compared with seeds'').
Candidates of class \textsc{erro} are the reference with one fact corrupted by
a programmatic rule --- a number replaced by a plausible other value (five
transformations), a named entity swapped for another entity of the corpus, or
a stated condition inverted (29 flips such as \emph{at least}/\emph{at most},
\emph{increases}/\emph{decreases}) --- so that what changed is known by
construction. The eight cases whose reference is the fixed abstention or
refusal phrase were excluded: there is no plausible wrong version of an
abstention, and paraphrases of a fixed sentence would measure the oracle's
ability to recognise that sentence rather than to judge an answer. The final
set has 247 pairs (111 \textsc{equiv}, 136 \textsc{erro}) over 22 cases.

\paragraph{Result.} The sweep returned $\tau^\ast = 0.60$, the minimum of the
grid, with $F_1 = 0.62$, precision 0.45, recall 1.0 and accuracy 0.449 (95\%
CI $[0.389, 0.510]$). The area under the ROC curve is 0.485. The maximum $F_1$
is obtained by accepting everything: no threshold separates a correct
paraphrase from an injected error. The mean similarity of the two classes is
0.970 for \textsc{equiv} and 0.967 for \textsc{erro}.

This is not a defect of measurement. Table~\ref{tab:controls} shows direct
controls on a single reference: a negated sentence scores above a legitimate
paraphrase, and a swapped number scores 0.918.

The reason is structural. An injected error is the reference with one token
changed, textually almost identical; a paraphrase is an honest rewrite with
another verb and another order. Cosine similarity measures surface proximity,
and therefore prefers the minimal corruption to the legitimate reformulation.
The defect this matters most for in RAG --- a wrong number in an otherwise
correct sentence --- is exactly the one the cheapest and most common oracle
does not see.

\paragraph{Consequences.} O1 is kept in the campaign at $\tau^\ast = 0.60$,
because the study measures what the oracles practitioners deploy detect, and
this is what that oracle detects. Its verdicts are read as such in
Section~\ref{sec:rq2}. Two limitations are declared. The \textsc{erro} class is
generated programmatically and may be easier to detect than the errors a model
makes naturally; and the reviewed paraphrases turned out conservative (a verb
and a connective changed, the structure kept). Both push in the same
direction: $\tau^\ast$ and the AUC are upper bounds on the oracle's
performance, from both sides. The same asymmetry that governed the retrieval
floor (Section~\ref{sec:instrumentation}) governs $\tau^\ast$: a threshold
that rejects a correct answer removes the case from every denominator
silently, a threshold that accepts a wrong one is visible in the results.

\section{Study Design}
\label{sec:design}

The design was registered before execution: operators, suite, oracles,
thresholds, repetitions, metrics and statistical tests were fixed and
versioned, and the deviations that the pilot forced are listed in
Section~\ref{sec:pilot}. We follow the reporting structure for case study
research in software engineering~\cite{runeson2009guidelines}, since the
object is one system studied in depth rather than a sample of systems.

\subsection{Research questions and metrics}
\label{sec:rqs}

\textbf{RQ1 --- Adequacy by layer.} Which layers of the RAG pipeline produce
defects that the suite fails to detect? The pre-registered hypothesis
(H1) is that surviving mutants concentrate in the corpus, chunking, index and
retrieval layers rather than in the prompt layer, because a suite that judges
the answer observes prompt defects directly and retrieval defects only through
their effect on the answer.

\textbf{RQ2 --- Oracle bias.} How much does the reported adequacy depend on the
oracle applied to identical artefacts? The hypothesis (H2) is that the spread
between oracles is of the same order as the twenty-point swing reported for
program-repair validators~\cite{bonfim2026evaluator}, and that disagreement
concentrates in the abstention and traceability classes, where a similarity
oracle and a judge disagree by nature.

\textbf{RQ3 --- Cost of rigour.} What does each level of rigour cost, and what
does it buy? The hypothesis (H3) is that cost grows faster than detection
across levels: repetition is what discounts non-determinism, and it is also
what costs.
\nota{Conferir a redacao de H1--H3 contra o protocolo pre-registrado; o
texto acima e a versao do relatorio de execucao.}

Let $V_o(m, c)$ be the verdict of oracle $o$ on case $c$ against mutant $m$,
aggregated over the $N_m$ runs: the majority verdict of the per-run verdicts
for O1, O2 and O5, and the verdict on the modal answer for O3 and O4. With
$\Sset_o$ as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:evaluable}, the \emph{kill rate} of a mutant is
the proportion of evaluable cases that fail on it,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:killrate}
\kr_o(m) = \frac{\bigl|\{\, c \in \Sset_o : V_o(m, c) = \fail \,\}\bigr|}{|\Sset_o|},
\end{equation}
reported with a Wilson 95\% confidence interval. A mutant is \emph{killed}
under $o$ when $\kr_o(m) > 0$, and the \emph{mutation score} of the suite
under $o$ is the proportion of killed mutants,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ms}
\MS_o = \frac{\bigl|\{\, m : \kr_o(m) > 0 \,\}\bigr|}{|M|}, \qquad
\MS^{\mathrm{coh}}_o = \frac{\bigl|\{\, m : \text{killed coherently under } o \,\}\bigr|}{|M|},
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the set of 18 mutants and a kill is \emph{coherent} when its
cause, classified as in Section~\ref{sec:coherence}, is one the operator was
designed to produce. The two readings are kept apart on purpose. With one
mutant per operator, a mutation score per operator would be 0 or 1 and its
confidence interval would say nothing; the kill rate over cases has a
denominator ($|\Sset_o|$) and an interpretable interval, and it is what enters
the per-layer comparison of RQ1. The mutation score is a property of the suite
under one oracle, and it is what the \emph{swing} of RQ2 compares:
$\mathrm{swing} = \max_o \MS_o - \min_o \MS_o$. For RQ3 the unit is the cost
of a level of rigour divided by the number of mutants it killed.

\subsection{Execution protocol}
\label{sec:execution}

The baseline is executed $N_b = 10$ times per case (300 invocations) and each
of the 18 mutants $N_m = 5$ times per case (2,700 invocations), for about
3,000 invocations of the assistant. Generation runs at temperature 0.0
everywhere except under P4, and the seed of run $r$ is $r$ itself, so that any
run can be named. Within each repetition the cases are executed in an order
shuffled with the repetition index as seed. The reason is a finding of the
second baseline (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}): at temperature 0 the seed changes
nothing, but the answer depends on the prompt that preceded it in the
server's slot, through prefix reuse in the KV cache. In a fixed order,
repetitions 2 to 10 of a case always follow the same predecessor and come
out byte-identical while repetition 1 differs; ten repetitions sample two
states. Shuffling gives every invocation a different predecessor, which is
what repetition is meant to do on a server that caches. The campaign does not stop at the first killing case: every
case runs against every mutant, because the cause of each death is a result
(Section~\ref{sec:coherence}) and stopping early would hide it. Within a
mutant, runs are ordered by repetition and then by case, so that the seed is
set once per repetition in the system configuration rather than per call; the
harness is resumable at the granularity of one invocation, and every invocation is appended to the log with the question,
the retrieved chunks and their similarity scores, the answer, the prompt and
completion token counts, wall-clock time, GPU-seconds and energy.

Oracles never run during the campaign. They read the log afterwards, and each
verdict records which oracle, which level of rigour and how many runs it was
derived from.

\subsection{Levels of rigour}
\label{sec:levels}

RQ3 compares three levels, all derived from the same log. \textbf{L1} is a
single run: the verdict of run 1 alone, with no evaluable set --- it is what a
protocol that executes each case once would have reported, and what most
suites in practice report. \textbf{L2} is the pre-registered protocol of the
campaign: $N_m = 5$ runs per (mutant, case) with the majority verdict, and
$\Sset_o$ from the ten baseline runs. \textbf{L3} adds 30 baseline runs on a
deterministic subset of ten cases, to estimate how much the stability
judgement of L2 would change with six times the repetitions. Because
execution is separated from judgement, moving between levels costs only the
oracles' time, which is measured; the generation cost of each level is the
cost of the runs it consumes.

\subsection{Coherence of kills}
\label{sec:coherence}

A kill is only evidence of adequacy if the case failed for the reason the
operator injected. Every kill $(m, c)$ is therefore classified by its cause
with a codebook of eight codes (Table~\ref{tab:codebook}), each with the set
of operators expected to produce it. A kill is \emph{coherent} when its code
is in the expected set of the operator that produced the mutant. The
classification is per kill, not per oracle: the cause is a property of the
answer, not of the instrument that rejected it. The codebook was frozen ---
date and author recorded in the versioned file --- before any campaign result
was read; a cause that fits no code is recorded as \textsc{other}, and a high
proportion of \textsc{other} is a result to report, not a problem to hide,
because it means the catalogue of causes was incomplete.

Reliability of the classification is measured in two ways. The first author
classifies every kill; 30\% of them, drawn at random, are re-classified blind
by the same author at least seven days later, and the intra-rater agreement is
reported as Cohen's $\kappa$. \nota{Se o segundo autor classificar uma amostra
independente, reportar tambem o kappa inter-avaliador; o protocolo preve
``dois classificadores''.} Agreement among oracles, which is a different
question, is reported as Fleiss' $\kappa$ in RQ2.

\subsection{Cost measurement}
\label{sec:cost}

Cost is measured, not estimated. For every invocation the harness records
wall-clock time, prompt and completion tokens as reported by the model server,
and, by sampling the GPU through the NVIDIA management library during the
call, GPU-seconds and energy in watt-hours. The oracles are metered in the
same way: the embedding calls of O1, the metric computations of O3 and the
judge calls of O4. The cost of a level of rigour is the sum over the runs and
verdicts that level consumes, and the cost per killed mutant divides it by the
mutants killed at that level under the primary oracle. Energy is reported as
measured on the study hardware and is not extrapolated to other devices.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:stats}

All tests were fixed before collection, at $\alpha = 0.05$; no proportion is
reported without a Wilson 95\% confidence interval and no difference of means
without a percentile-bootstrap 95\% interval (10,000 resamples, fixed seed).
For RQ1, the per-operator kill rates are grouped by layer and compared with a
Kruskal--Wallis test, with $\eta^2$ as effect size and Dunn's post-hoc
comparisons under Holm's correction~\cite{holm1979simple}. For RQ2, the binary
verdicts of the five oracles on the same (mutant, case) pairs are compared with
Cochran's $Q$~\cite{cochran1950comparison}, followed by pairwise McNemar tests
under Holm's correction, and their agreement is summarised by Fleiss' $\kappa$
and by pairwise Cohen's $\kappa$; disagreement is also broken down by test
class. For RQ3, the cost per killed mutant at each level is reported with its
bootstrap interval, and detection and cost are plotted on separate panels with
a shared abscissa rather than on a dual axis, so that the two slopes can be
compared without suggesting that they share a unit. The analysis script that
produces every table and figure was run on synthetic data of known structure
before the campaign, to expose edge cases without spending GPU time on them.

\subsection{Pilot and deviations from the registered protocol}
\label{sec:pilot}

Before the campaign, three cases (a direct fact, a distributed fact and an
out-of-corpus question) were run twice each against the baseline and against
two mutants chosen to kill them (R1 and P2): 18 invocations, 45 verdicts,
all five oracles. The pilot validated the path from execution to verdict with
cost measured end to end, and forced the following deviations from the
registered protocol, all made before any mutant result was examined.

\emph{Retrieval check on the suite.} The question of one direct-fact case
retrieved four documents, none of them the one holding its evidence; in a
corpus where every document discusses testing, a generically worded question
retrieves a topical neighbour. The check of Section~\ref{sec:suite} was added,
found two more, and the three questions were rewritten. Three out-of-corpus
questions were also replaced, for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:instrumentation}.

\emph{Context window.} The first full baseline (300 invocations, 2.33\,h)
failed 24 of the 30 cases under the deterministic oracle and 7 under the
similarity oracle, and every verdict pointed at the system: answers from
parametric knowledge on the out-of-corpus questions, no citations, ``the
text does not mention'' with the evidence in the retrieved context. The cause
was in the model server's log, not in the verdicts: 288 silent truncations of
the prompt to 2,050 tokens (Section~\ref{sec:instrumentation}). The run and
its verdicts were archived, not discarded, and repeated with the window sized
per configuration. Three of the conclusions drawn from the first run were
retracted by the second: with the full prompt the assistant answered the case
it had declared unanswerable, abstained with the exact marker on the
out-of-corpus case, and cited the source with document and page.

\emph{Deterministic oracle and the second baseline.} The second baseline
(300 invocations, no truncation, 27\,s and 0.368\,Wh per invocation) gave
$|\Sset_{O1}| = 25$, $|\Sset_{O4}| = 23$ and $|\Sset_{O2}| = |\Sset_{O5}| =
21$ after three corrections to the instruments, each made on the baseline
verdicts and before any mutant: the deterministic oracle now treats a decimal
comma and a decimal point between digits as the same character (a correct
``3.79\%'' failed against the reference's ``3,79\%''); two assertions that
matched an exact inflection or a fixed preposition were relaxed to stems;
and the retrieval check was extended once more, from page to value, after a
case retrieved the right page but the chunk without the number. The five
out-of-corpus cases abstain with the exact marker in 10 of 10 runs, which
settles the question left open by the truncated baseline: the abstention
class stays and P2 and R4 are evaluable. The nine cases outside
$\Sset_{O5}$ are three accepted retrieval gaps, one case whose table has
nine near-identical siblings in the same document, one incomplete answer
(two of three operators listed), the three ambiguous cases --- the
assistant abstains instead of asking, because its prompt has no
clarification rule --- and one traceability case in which the generator
copied the example of the citation rule literally.

\emph{Repetition is not an independent sample.} In the second baseline,
14 cases had ten identical answers and 15 had repetitions 2 to 10 identical
to one another and different from repetition 1 --- not by a token, but by a
rewrite (similarity ratio 0.38 to 0.80, same facts). Six probe invocations
showed that the seed has no effect at temperature 0, that an identical
prompt within one model load yields an identical answer, and that the answer
changes with the prompt that preceded it in the server's slot. Ten
repetitions in a fixed order therefore sample two states. The order of cases
is now shuffled per repetition (Section~\ref{sec:execution}), the baseline
was run a third time under that rule, and the fixed-order baseline is kept
in the package so that the two estimates of stability can be compared.

R4 & retrieval & 0 & 0.000 [0.000; 0.161] \\

Grouped by layer, the kill rates do not differ: index 0.225, prompt 0.200,
retrieval 0.163, corpus 0.150, chunking 0.150, with Kruskal--Wallis
$H = 1.368$, $p = 0.850$ over five groups of four operators or fewer, and no
pairwise comparison surviving Holm's correction. \textbf{H1 is not supported};
with at most four operators per layer the test has little power, so the honest
reading is that this design cannot separate the layers, not that the layers are
equivalent.

Two operators deserve their own account, because in both the number hides the
mechanism.

\emph{R4 survives for a reason the corpus explains.} The five cases that target
it are the out-of-corpus questions, and Section~\ref{sec:instrumentation}
already showed that in a topically homogeneous corpus those questions retrieve
four chunks well above any floor that preserves the legitimate cases. Removing
the floor therefore changes nothing: the mutant is equivalent \emph{on this
corpus}, and the survivor is a property of the corpus and of the suite, not a
gap in the assistant's testing. A study that reported R4 as an undetected
defect would be reporting an artefact.

\emph{E1 does not degrade retrieval, it stops it.} Swapping the embedding model
for a smaller one from another family leaves 95 of its 150 invocations with
\emph{zero} retrieved chunks and a median prompt of 302 tokens, against 4,646
in the baseline. The cause is the relevance floor: 0.428 was calibrated on the
similarity scale of one model, and the other model's scale is different. The
floor is a property of the pair (corpus, embedding model), not of the corpus,
and a routine model upgrade invalidates it silently. This is the finding of
Section~\ref{sec:instrumentation} seen from the other side: there the floor
failed to reject a question it should have rejected; here it rejects the
evidence it should have kept. A global similarity floor is fragile in both
directions, and the fragility appears only when something apparently unrelated
to it changes.

\subsection{RQ2 --- Oracle bias}
\label{sec:rq2}

Two of the oracles had their bias established before the campaign, on artefacts
that do not depend on it. They are reported first because they bound what the
campaign numbers can mean.

\paragraph{O1 inverts on the defects that matter most.} On the 247 calibration
pairs of Section~\ref{sec:calibration}, cosine similarity to the reference
ranks an answer with one number swapped above a correct paraphrase in 75\% of
comparisons (AUC 0.248) and an answer with an inverted condition above a
correct paraphrase in 91\% (AUC 0.091); it separates entity substitutions
(AUC 0.808). The pattern holds on a second embedding model of another family
(AUC 0.140 and 0.174 for numbers and conditions). Any threshold accepts or
rejects paraphrases and numeric errors together; at the calibrated
$\tau^\ast = 0.60$ it accepts both.

\paragraph{O3 is blind to fabrication about absent topics.} On the pilot
artefacts, the faithfulness metric of the RAGAS bundle scored 1.0 for an answer
that fabricated a figure about a topic the retrieved context does not mention,
under both available implementations and under a judge twice the size of the
one registered (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}). The metric asks whether the answer's
claims contradict the context, and a claim about an absent topic contradicts
nothing. The defect that operator P2 injects --- answering instead of
abstaining --- is invisible to it by definition. O3 was therefore dropped from
the campaign under the contingency of Section~\ref{sec:design}, and the
campaign ran with four oracles.

\paragraph{The campaign.} Over the 342 (mutant, case) pairs that are evaluable
under all four oracles, the four disagree
(Cochran's $Q = 58.62$, $p = 1.2 \times 10^{-12}$), with Fleiss'
$\kappa = 0.681$ --- substantial agreement in the usual reading, and still a
spread of \textbf{22.2 percentage points} in the reported mutation score
(Table~\ref{tab:oraclebias}). The same suite, the same 3,000 invocations, the
same artefacts: 0.722 under the cheapest oracle and 0.944 under the
deterministic one. The swing is of the same order as the twenty points reported
for program-repair validators~\cite{bonfim2026evaluator} on a different task,
which suggests the effect is a property of reference-based evaluation rather
than of this pipeline.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{An adequacy score is a property of the pair (suite, oracle)}

\todo{Abrir com o swing observado.} Before any campaign number, the calibration
already shows that the same suite, over the same artefacts, would report
different adequacy under O1 and O4 for every mutant that changes a value: O1
cannot fail a wrong number and O4 fails it on every control. A mutation score
for a RAG assistant reported without the oracle that produced it is not
interpretable, and we suggest that studies of LLM-based systems report
adequacy per oracle as a matter of course, as program-repair studies have begun
to report effectiveness per validator~\cite{bonfim2026evaluator}.

\subsection{What the cheapest oracle is good for}

Cosine similarity to a reference is not useless; it is mis-specified as a
correctness oracle. On our data it separates topic with a wide margin and is
nearly blind to value and polarity. It therefore detects the failures that
change \emph{what} the answer is about --- an abstention where an answer was
due, a drift to a neighbouring subject, a refusal --- and misses the failures
that change what the answer \emph{says}. The practical form is the conjunction
of Section~\ref{sec:o5}: a similarity gate for drift and deterministic
assertions for every value the case depends on. Where a reference answer is
available, the numbers in it are the assertions; where it is not, the metric
bundle's faithfulness is not a substitute, because it cannot see a claim about
what the context does not contain.

\subsection{The reference answer is the oracle}

Three of the checks added during this study exist because a reference answer
is not documentation but an instrument. A reference with a justification the
model appended to a correct fact penalises a correct answer
(Section~\ref{sec:suite}); an assertion that its own reference does not
satisfy removes the case from the analysis silently (Section~\ref{sec:o2}); a
judge that reads the reference as a ceiling fails answers better than it
(Section~\ref{sec:o4}). All three are invisible until the oracle is run
against the unmutated system, and all three bias the result in the same
direction --- towards failing the baseline, which under Eq.~\eqref{eq:evaluable}
shrinks the denominator without raising an error. The single most useful
practice we can recommend to whoever builds a suite for a RAG assistant is
to run every oracle against the baseline before trusting it against anything
else.

\subsection{Mutation operators need a corpus criterion}

A mutation operator for source code carries its own observability: a changed
operator is exercised if the line is executed. A mutation operator for a
corpus does not. Whether truncating a document removes anything the suite can
ask about, whether a chunk-size change moves any boundary, whether a
similarity floor rejects anything at all, depend on the corpus, the pagination
and the baseline configuration (Section~\ref{sec:equivalence}). Three of our
eighteen operators were inert on the first version of the corpus and none of
them would have reported an error; they would have survived, and the survival
would have read as a gap in the suite. A catalogue of RAG operators is
therefore only half of the instrument; the other half is the set of
measurements --- fact density per page, chunks per page, distribution of
similarities of the negative cases --- that establish, before the campaign,
that each operator has something to act on.

\subsection{Homogeneous corpora and the relevance floor}

An assistant over a corpus of one domain is the common industrial case: the
regulations of one agency, the manuals of one product, the papers of one
field. In such a corpus every question is on-topic, including the ones whose
answer is absent, and a global similarity floor cannot tell coverage from
topicality (Section~\ref{sec:instrumentation}). The consequence for testing is
that the out-of-corpus class of a suite exercises the abstention instruction
of the prompt much more than the retrieval floor, so that a suite which
appears to test retrieval fallback is in fact testing prompt compliance; the
consequence for engineering is that abstention has to be a property of
generation, not of retrieval, in any corpus of this shape.

\subsection{The parameter nobody declares}

The context window of the model server is not in the RAG literature's list of
parameters to control, and it was not in our protocol. It decided the outcome
of 318 invocations before we read a log line. A suite that judges answers
cannot see it, because the model answers fluently from what it remembers when
the rules and the evidence are cut away; a fidelity check on the operators
cannot see it, because the operators worked --- on a prompt the model never
read. The only instrument that would have caught it on day one is the one we
added on week six: a sentinel rule in the system prompt whose absence in the
answer means the prompt did not arrive. We suggest it as a standing check for
any study of an LLM-based system, alongside the declaration of the window
itself.

\subsection{Cost, and where it goes}

\todo{Completar com T5.} One observation is already fixed by the pilot: with a
generator that emits a reasoning trace, the cost of the system under test
dominates the cost of every oracle, judge included. The protocol's expectation
that O4 would be the expensive oracle and O1 the cheap one remains true
between oracles, but both are second-order beside generation, and repetition
--- the only known discount for non-determinism --- multiplies the dominant
term. Whether that price buys detection is what Section~\ref{sec:rq3}
measures.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

We follow the usual four categories~\cite{runeson2009guidelines}, and
distinguish the threats we anticipated from the ones the study demonstrated
--- the latter are stated with the evidence, because a demonstrated threat is
also a result.

\paragraph{Construct validity.} The central construct is ``the suite detects
the defect'', and it is operationalised by an oracle. Section~\ref{sec:calibration}
shows that the cheapest oracle does not measure correctness on this corpus but
topical proximity, and Section~\ref{sec:pilot} that the standard metric bundle
does not measure fabrication about absent topics. We do not treat these as
threats to be mitigated but as findings, and we mitigate the construct
threat itself by reporting adequacy per oracle and never as one number. Two
instruments remain upper bounds: $\tau^\ast$ was calibrated on
programmatically corrupted references and conservative paraphrases, so the
oracle's performance in the field can only be worse than measured; and the
judge sees the reference and not the document, so it can only detect
contradiction, not fabrication compatible with the reference. The reference
answers are themselves a construct: they were drafted by a model and checked
against the source one by one, six were rewritten to remove justifications
the model had added, and one source sentence turned out to be truncated by a
figure caption in the normalised corpus. The relevance floor of the baseline
was calibrated on the same suite it gates; the margin below the weakest
positive case was chosen so that the error it can make is the visible one.

\paragraph{Internal validity.} A kill must be attributable to the injected
defect. Three mechanisms address this: the fidelity check of every operator
(Section~\ref{sec:fidelity}), which rules out a mutant that changes nothing;
the residue check, which rules out a mutant that changes the next one; and the
evaluable set (Section~\ref{sec:equivalence}), which rules out kills on cases
the unmutated system does not answer stably. The coherence classification
addresses the remaining attribution question --- did the case fail for the
injected reason --- and its codebook was frozen before any result was read.
The judge is of a different family from the generator, against
self-preference~\cite{panickssery2024llm}, and the paraphraser of the
calibration set is of a different family from the generator, against
circularity; both are of the same family as each other, which is a residual
risk we declare. The pilot and the first baseline forced changes to the suite, to the
judge's prompt and to the model server's context window before the campaign;
all are listed in Section~\ref{sec:pilot} and none was made after a mutant
result was examined. The truncated baseline is the strongest internal threat
the study met, and it was not caught by any oracle, gate or test: every
instrument judged the answer, and the defect was in what the model was given.
We report it because a replication on another server or model would meet the
same silent default, and because the guard that now catches it costs nothing. The run that revealed the
unmutated system fabricating an answer is a threat of a different kind: the
class of test cases that requires abstention presupposes a baseline that
abstains, and where it does not, the operator that removes abstention cannot
be observed. The evaluable set keeps such cases out of every denominator, at
the price of leaving that operator with fewer cases; the alternative, moving
the questions further from the corpus until the system abstains, would test
less of what a user would actually ask.

\paragraph{External validity.} The study is of one assistant, one corpus of
eight documents in one domain, one 30-case suite, and one pair of open-weight
models of 8 and 4 billion parameters on consumer hardware. The catalogue and
the protocol are meant to transfer; the numbers are not. Two of the findings
were checked for dependence on the instrument and held: the inversion of the
similarity oracle replicates on a second embedding model of another family,
and the blind spot of the metric bundle persists under a larger judge. The
corpus is topically homogeneous, which is the common industrial case and also
what defeats a global relevance floor; a heterogeneous corpus would move
detections from the prompt layer to the retrieval layer and the RQ1 result
should be read with that in mind. Larger or commercial models would change the
baseline stability, the cost figures and, most likely, the propensity to
fabricate; they would not change what the oracles can and cannot see, which
is the part of the result we expect to generalise.

\paragraph{Conclusion validity.} There is one mutant per operator, so the
mutation score per operator is a single binary observation; every inferential
statement is made on the kill rate over cases, whose denominator is
$|\Sset_o|$ and whose interval is reported. The suite has 30 cases and the
classes have between three and six; the per-layer comparison groups four
operators or fewer, and Kruskal--Wallis on groups of that size has low power,
which we report as such rather than compensate. C4 is targeted by a single
case and any statement about it rests on one observation. The five
repetitions per mutant are not independent draws from the system's
distribution of answers: at temperature 0 they differ only through the state
of the server's cache, which the shuffled order varies but does not
randomise; the stability they measure is a lower bound on the instability a
deployment would see across sessions. All multiple
comparisons are corrected by Holm's procedure, all tests were fixed before
collection, and the analysis code was validated on synthetic data of known
structure before being run on the campaign.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\todo{Escrever depois da campanha. Estrutura: (i) a pergunta --- que camadas
uma suite tipica nao ve; (ii) a resposta da RQ1 em uma frase com numero; (iii)
o resultado da RQ2: o swing observado e as duas inversoes estabelecidas antes
da campanha; (iv) o custo por mutante morto e o que L2 compra sobre L1; (v) o
que fica para quem replica: catalogo, criterio de adequacao do corpus,
conjunto avaliavel, e a regra de reportar adequacao por oraculo.}

Mutation testing can be transposed to retrieval-augmented assistants, but not
by analogy. The operators have to act on the corpus, the chunking, the index
and the retrieval, where the documented failures are; each has to carry a
check that it acts at all; and the score has to be computed on the cases the
unmutated system answers stably, because there is no other ground on which a
kill can be attributed. What the transposition then reveals is that the
number depends on who is asked: the oracle that practitioners deploy most
widely cannot see a wrong number in a correct sentence, and the metric that
the field treats as standard cannot see a fabrication about a topic the
context does not contain. \todo{Ultima frase: o que a campanha mostrou que a
suite nao ve.}
